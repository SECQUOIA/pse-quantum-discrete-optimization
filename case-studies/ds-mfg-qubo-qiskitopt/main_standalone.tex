\renewcommand{\PaperAbstract}{%
Discrete decisions, such as selecting routes, units, and operating modes, are
an early and combinatorially hard step in pharmaceutical process design.
Gate-based quantum optimizers, the quantum approximate optimization algorithm
(QAOA) and the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE), are increasingly proposed
for such problems, which raises a practical question for the process systems
engineering community. Once a discrete-design model is mapped to the quadratic
unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) form these methods require, do the
resulting samples actually correspond to process-design decisions, and how do
they compare with classical methods? We study this question on a small but
fully enumerable drug-substance manufacturing instance with 19 binary flow
decisions. The penalty reformulation needed to obtain a QUBO adds 17 auxiliary
variables, so a sampled 36-bit state is not itself a design decision: it becomes
one only after projection onto the 19 flow variables and exact repair of the
auxiliaries. A quadratic surrogate, needed to feed the QUBO interfaces, fits the
repaired landscape almost perfectly ($R^2 \approx 0.999$) yet is unreliable as a
minimizer. After this preprocessing, energy-targeted transferred-angle QAOA
concentrates probability on the optimal flowsheet (664 of 262144 ideal reads),
but does not outperform a simple classical hill climber, an exact solver, or
exhaustive enumeration. A small IBM hardware run completes the same pipeline
without adding an advantage. We report these results as a workflow-feasibility
and interpretation study, not as a claim of quantum speedup.
}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:ds-mfg-introduction}

Discrete choices are often the first barrier in process design. Before a
flowsheet can be simulated or optimized over continuous variables, the designer
must commit to a set of mutually exclusive options: which synthesis route to
use, which unit operations to include, and which operating modes to run
\cite{Turkay1996,Mencarelli_2020}. In pharmaceutical manufacturing these choices
are particularly consequential, because route and equipment selection shape
cost, yield, and regulatory burden, and because digital design and optimization
tools for this sector are comparatively recent
\cite{CasasOrozco2021,Laky2022}. The resulting models are combinatorial, and even
modestly sized superstructures generate large discrete search spaces.

Gate-based quantum optimization is one of the emerging tools proposed for this
setting. QAOA and VQE are designed to sample low-energy binary states
\cite{Farhi2014,Peruzzo2014,Cerezo2021}, which makes them a natural candidate
for discrete selection problems. Current hardware is noisy and of intermediate
scale \cite{Preskill2018}, and recent surveys of quantum optimization stress
that careful benchmarking against classical methods is essential and that a
practical quantum advantage for optimization has not yet been established
\cite{Abbas2024}. The relevant near-term question is therefore not whether such
methods will eventually surpass classical solvers, but what is required to use
them today and how their output compares with established classical baselines.

Within chemical and process systems engineering, quantum computing has been
reviewed as an emerging capability \cite{BernalAIChE2022,Nourbakhsh2022}, and
hybrid quantum-classical strategies have been proposed for discrete and
discrete-continuous optimization in process and energy systems
\cite{AjagekarYou2019,Ajagekar2020}. Case studies have begun to map process- and
molecular-design problems to the quadratic unconstrained binary optimization
(QUBO) form that these methods require
\cite{Iftakher,glover2019,Lucas2014,Glover2022}, and discrete process-design
instances have been solved with Ising-based annealing hardware
\cite{park2026arxiv,Ajagekar2020}. These works establish that the mapping is
possible. Fewer examine what happens to the samples after the reformulation, and
the gate-based circuit path is less explored than annealing for these problems. Casting a constrained discrete problem as a QUBO
requires penalty terms and, in general, additional binary variables, and the way
this is done affects both the penalty weights and the variable count
\cite{Quintero2022}. Those auxiliary variables are bookkeeping rather than design
decisions, so a raw quantum sample mixes the two and is not directly
interpretable. The question we ask is therefore concrete: for a discrete
pharmaceutical design instance, what does it take to turn gate-based quantum
samples into process-design decisions, and once that is done, how do QAOA and VQE
compare with classical sampling and with exact solution?

We answer this through an end-to-end audit on a single drug-substance
manufacturing (DS-MFG) instance, chosen to be small enough to enumerate exactly
and audit completely, yet large enough to expose the reformulation and
interpretation issues that arise at scale. The instance is a
capital-expenditure selection model with 19 binary flow variables; its QUBO form
has 36 variables after 17 auxiliaries are added. Our contribution is not a new
QAOA or VQE variant. It is a transparent account of the interpretation pipeline
and an honest comparison: (i) a sampled QUBO state becomes a design candidate
only after projection to the 19 flow variables and exact auxiliary repair;
(ii) the quadratic surrogate needed to feed the QUBO interfaces fits the
repaired landscape globally but fails as a minimizer; (iii) after this
preprocessing, transferred-angle QAOA places measurable probability on the
optimal flowsheet but does not beat a simple hill climber or the exact solver;
and (iv) a fixed-circuit IBM hardware run completes the pipeline without adding
an advantage.

For the process systems engineering community, the value of this study is a
concrete, reproducible measurement of the engineering burden that sits between a
discrete-design model and a usable quantum sample, on a problem from a relevant
application domain. The conclusion is deliberately conservative: the results
demonstrate feasibility and interpretation, not a runtime or hardware advantage
over classical computation.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:ds-mfg-methods}

\subsection{The Discrete Design Instance}
\label{subsec:ds-mfg-instance}

The DS-MFG instance is the discrete subproblem of a drug-substance manufacturing
process-optimization case study introduced by Park and Bernal
Neira~\cite{park2026arxiv}. There, a mixed-integer nonlinear design problem is
decomposed so that the discrete part is handed to Ising-based annealing solvers
(simulated annealing, quantum annealing, and entropy computing) while the
nonlinear part is handled by simulators and continuous solvers. We keep the same
discrete instance but ask what is required to run gate-based optimizers on it
instead. The instance is a binary integer program for capital-expenditure
selection in the manufacturing superstructure: the 19 binary
variables encode candidate material-flow arcs and unit-operation selections, and
the objective is a normalized capital-cost score. Schematically, the discrete
problem has the form
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{f} \quad & \sum_{i \in F} c_i f_i \\
\text{s.t.}\quad
& \sum_{i \in \mathrm{In}(n)} f_i
  = \sum_{j \in \mathrm{Out}(n)} f_j
    && \forall n \in N, \\
& f_{\mathrm{source}} = 1,\quad f_{\mathrm{sink}} = 1,\\
& g(f) \leq 0,\\
& f_i \in \{0,1\}
    && \forall i \in F .
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ds-mfg-ip-formulation}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:ds-mfg-ip-formulation} lists the relevant constraint classes
rather than the full instance: flow balance at each node $n \in N$, fixed source
and sink selections, and additional logical constraints $g(f) \leq 0$ that
enforce a valid flowsheet.

Solved with Gurobi \cite{gurobi}, the model (19 binary variables, 20
constraints) returns an optimal capital-cost score of 11.7095 in 0.073 s, and
its solution pool retains 36 feasible flows. A rerun with a wider pool setting
reproduces the same optimum at zero MIP gap. We therefore treat 11.7095 and its
corresponding flow as a certified global optimum and use it as the reference
throughout. We record whether a sampled flow matches one of the 36 pooled
solutions; this is a pool-membership check, not a feasibility certificate for
the flows outside the pool.

\subsection{From Integer Program to QUBO and Back}
\label{subsec:ds-mfg-qubo-repair}

To run QAOA and VQE, the integer program must be expressed as a QUBO, in which
all constraints are folded into a single quadratic objective over binary
variables \cite{glover2019,Lucas2014,Glover2022}. Equality and inequality
constraints are moved into the objective through penalty terms, and inequalities
in particular require auxiliary (slack) variables. The penalty weights and the
number of auxiliaries are not unique, and both affect how faithfully the QUBO
represents the original problem \cite{Quintero2022}. For this instance the
penalty reformulation introduces 17 auxiliary variables, so the QUBO acts on 36
binary variables. For a bitstring $x \in \{0,1\}^{36}$, the encoded objective is
\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{QUBO}}(x)
    =
    \ell^\top x + x^\top Qx + b,
    \label{eq:ds-mfg-full-qubo}
\end{equation}
with linear coefficients $\ell$, quadratic coefficient matrix $Q$, and constant
offset $b$; off-diagonal couplings are stored in the upper triangle of $Q$ and
counted once.

The 36 variables separate into the original flow variables and the auxiliaries,
\[
    x = (y,z), \qquad
    y \in \{0,1\}^{19}, \quad z \in \{0,1\}^{17}.
\]
Only $y$ encodes a design decision; $z$ is bookkeeping introduced by the
reformulation. A sampled 36-bit string therefore does not map directly to a
flowsheet. We make it interpretable by projecting onto $y$ and then repairing
the auxiliaries, that is, choosing the auxiliary assignment that minimizes the
QUBO energy for that flow,
\begin{equation}
    F(y)
    =
    \min_{z \in \{0,1\}^{17}} E_{\mathrm{QUBO}}(y,z).
    \label{eq:ds-mfg-repaired-objective}
\end{equation}
The repaired objective $F(y)$ is the score we compare against the
integer-program objective, and the repair itself is a classical subproblem
embedded in the workflow. It is exact and cheap here because, once $y$ is fixed,
the auxiliary-auxiliary coupling graph splits into 13 small connected components
of one or two variables each, so each component is solved by enumeration. This
decomposition is a property of the instance; for a general QUBO the auxiliary
repair is itself NP-hard, a limitation we return to in
Section~\ref{sec:ds-mfg-discussion}.

This repair gives a valid bridge back to the integer program. The exact repaired
minimizer over all $2^{19}$ flows is the certified Gurobi optimum, and across the
36 pooled flows the repaired QUBO scores match the integer-program objective to
within $3.1\times 10^{-13}$. That agreement confirms that the QUBO and the
integer program encode the same objective. We separately enumerate all $2^{19}$
flows and rank them by repaired objective to obtain reference sets such as the
exact top-10 and top-50. Because Gurobi's pool of 36 is not exhaustive, 14 of the
top-50 repaired flows are not in the pool, which is why pool membership alone
does not certify a flow as good or bad.

The repaired objective $F(y)$ is not a quadratic function of $y$, but the QUBO
interfaces used by QAOA and VQE require one. We therefore fit a 19-variable
quadratic surrogate
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{F}(y)
    =
    \widehat{b}
    + \widehat{\ell}^{\top} y
    + y^{\top} \widehat{Q} y
    \label{eq:ds-mfg-quadratic-surrogate}
\end{equation}
by least squares over all $2^{19}$ repaired flows. The surrogate fits the
landscape almost perfectly in aggregate ($R^2 \approx 0.999$), but this global
accuracy is misleading for optimization: the surrogate's own minimizer repairs
to 14.5505 rather than the true 11.7095, the true optimum ranks only 33rd under
the surrogate, and among the exact top-50 flows the surrogate's absolute errors
reach 29.4. We therefore use $\widehat{F}$ only to generate variational samples;
every reported sample-quality metric is computed with the exact repaired
objective $F$, not $\widehat{F}$. The gap between global fit and ranking accuracy
is itself one of the lessons of this study.

\subsection{Quantum, Classical, and Hardware Workflows}
\label{subsec:ds-mfg-workflows}

We evaluate several workflows over this pipeline. The local QAOA and VQE
workflows run through QiskitOpt.jl, a Julia wrapper around Qiskit Optimization
and Qiskit Aer
\cite{JavadiAbhari2024,QiskitAerSoftware,QiskitOptimizationSoftware,QiskitOptSoftware},
with QUBO models constructed through the QUBO.jl ecosystem \cite{toqubo:2023}.
Direct full-QUBO QAOA and VQE
operate on the 36-variable formulation; reduced-surrogate QAOA and VQE operate
on the 19-variable surrogate and are scored by exact repair. The strongest
quantum workflow adds an offline angle-search step: QAOA angles are optimized on
the surrogate with JuliQAOA \cite{Golden2023JuliQAOA}, and the fixed angles are
then transferred to a Qiskit circuit for sampling. We
distinguish two angle objectives. The energy-targeted objective minimizes the
expected surrogate energy,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{energy}}(\theta)
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{y \sim p_{\theta}}\!\left[\widehat{F}(y)\right],
    \label{eq:ds-mfg-energy-target}
\end{equation}
and uses no knowledge of the optimal flow; we call it non-oracle because the
angle search does not see the answer. The top-10-targeted objective maximizes
probability mass on the enumerated exact top-10 repaired flows,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{J}_{10}(\theta)
    =
    \sum_{y \in \mathcal{T}_{10}} p_{\theta}(y),
    \label{eq:ds-mfg-top10-target}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}_{10}$ is that enumerated set. This objective is oracle-aided,
because it uses the known ranking, and we report it only as a diagnostic upper
bound on what fixed-angle QAOA can concentrate, not as a deployable strategy.
Reduced-surrogate VQE uses an \texttt{EfficientSU2} ansatz optimized with COBYLA.

As classical baselines we sample the repaired 19-flow objective by uniform
random sampling and by single-bit-flip hill climbing with random restarts. As a
hardware feasibility test we run the fixed top-10-targeted QAOA circuit on the
IBM \texttt{ibm\_fez} backend. An independent statevector calculation predicts
the central QAOA reference-optimum probability at $2.63\times 10^{-3}$,
consistent with the $2.53\times 10^{-3}$ sampled rate, which checks the simulator
against the sampler. Software versions, random seeds, simulator settings (Aer's
matrix-product-state method \cite{Vidal2003,Schollwoeck2011}), circuit depths,
and job identifiers are recorded in the artifact bundle so that the cached
results can be inspected and rescored; we keep them out of the main text and
summarize only the settings needed to read the results.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:ds-mfg-metrics}

For each sampled state $x=(y,z)$ we record the raw QUBO energy
$E_{\mathrm{QUBO}}(y,z)$, the repaired objective $F(y)$, and the auxiliary gap
$E_{\mathrm{QUBO}}(y,z)-F(y)$. A reference-optimum hit means the projected and
repaired flow equals the certified optimal flow; it does not require the 36-bit
sample to also contain the optimal auxiliary assignment. We report
reference-optimum hits, top-10 and top-50 repaired-rank hits, and pooled-flow
hits, with Wilson 95\% intervals. We also report empirical time-to-target
diagnostics \cite{Bernal2024-benchmarking}, with the important caveat that these
count reads or evaluations only and exclude the substantial offline costs of
enumeration, surrogate fitting, angle search, transpilation, and queueing.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:ds-mfg-results}

\subsection{Repair Changes the Meaning of a Sample}
\label{subsec:ds-mfg-repair-meaning}

Sampling the 36-variable QUBO directly does not yield an interpretable design
result. A $p=2$ full-QUBO QAOA run produced no reference-optimum reads in 512
final samples, and a higher-read full-QUBO run found the repaired optimum only 4
times in 32768 reads, and then only as a repaired-flow hit rather than as a
fully optimal 36-bit completion. These runs are not performance claims; they
show concretely that without projection and repair, a raw QUBO sample cannot be
read as a flowsheet. Figure~\ref{fig:ds-mfg-repair-pipeline} (left) summarizes
the scoring pipeline that the remaining results assume: the flow variables
define the candidate, and auxiliary repair defines the score.

\subsection{Reduction and Angle Transfer Concentrate QAOA Samples}
\label{subsec:ds-mfg-qaoa-main}

Once the pipeline is in place, reduction to the surrogate and transferred-angle
search are what make QAOA samples concentrate on good flowsheets. The primary
non-oracle result is the energy-targeted $p=5$ transferred workflow, which
sampled the certified optimum 664 times in 262144 ideal reads (rate
$2.53\times 10^{-3}$, Wilson 95\% interval $[2.35,2.73]\times 10^{-3}$), together
with 39661 top-50 and 8964 top-10 reads. In design terms, roughly one in 400
reads lands on the optimal selection, and a large fraction land among the 50 best
flowsheets. The oracle-aided top-10-targeted objective raises this to 1007
optimum reads in the same budget (62597 top-50 and 14326 top-10 reads), which
bounds how sharply a fixed-angle circuit can be made to concentrate but uses the
enumerated ranking and so is not deployable.

\subsection{VQE and Classical Baselines Bound the Claim}
\label{subsec:ds-mfg-vqe-classical-main}

VQE benefits from the same reduction but produces a flatter distribution.
Full-QUBO VQE did not sample the optimum in a five-seed sweep; reduced-surrogate
VQE reached a near-optimal pooled flow (objective 11.8105) and, for a selected
seed, sampled the certified optimum 20 times in 524288 reads. The classical
baselines keep the comparison honest. Uniform random sampling never found the
optimum, which is expected: with one optimum among $2^{19}$ flows, the expected
count in 262144 draws is about 0.5. Hill climbing with random restarts was much
stronger, finding the optimum 19 times and reaching 883 top-50 flows in 262144
repaired-objective evaluations. At matched nominal counts, QAOA places more
probability on low-rank flows than these baselines, but the counts do not
normalize the preprocessing, angle search, simulation, or solver effort behind
each method, so this is a statement about sample concentration, not about
end-to-end cost.

\subsection{Hardware Execution Is a Feasibility Handoff}
\label{subsec:ds-mfg-hardware-main}

The fixed top-10-targeted QAOA circuit ran on \texttt{ibm\_fez} across three
repeats and three transpile seeds (92001--92003), 4096 shots each, for 36864
hardware reads in total. The run completed and produced 6 top-50 reads, 1 top-10
read, and 4 pooled-flow reads, with no reference-optimum read; the best hardware
sample was a rank-2 pooled flow (objective 11.8105). With zero optimum hits the
Wilson 95\% upper bound on the hardware optimum-hit rate is about
$1.04\times 10^{-4}$. Because calibration and full transpilation metadata were
not retained, we use this run only to confirm that the submission-and-scoring
path executes end to end on hardware, not to attribute the lower hit rate to a
specific noise mechanism. The result is a feasibility handoff, not evidence of a
hardware advantage.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:ds-mfg-discussion}

This study answers a narrower but more useful question than whether quantum
optimization solves the DS-MFG instance, which Gurobi already does in well under
a second. The useful finding is what has to happen for a QAOA or VQE
distribution to yield meaningful process-design samples: a reformulation that
separates design variables from auxiliaries, exact repair to recover a valid
objective, a surrogate to satisfy the QUBO interface, an offline angle search,
backend-specific execution, and scoring back in the original flow space. The
positive result is that, after all of this, the repaired sampling distribution
concentrates on good flowsheets; the negative result is that this concentration
does not translate into a runtime or hardware advantage over classical methods
on this instance.

The main limitations follow directly. Exact auxiliary repair is tractable here
only because the conditioned auxiliary graph decomposes; in general it is
NP-hard, and an approximate repair would change the meaning of every reported
metric. Constraint-preserving mixers are an alternative to the penalty-plus-repair
route, since they keep the quantum search within the feasible subspace and can
avoid some auxiliary variables \cite{Hadfield2019}; whether they reduce the
interpretation burden for process-design QUBOs at scale is an open question. More
broadly, the repair step places this workflow within hybrid quantum-classical
optimization, where quantum sampling is interleaved with classical solution of
structured subproblems \cite{Ajagekar2020,Brown2024}. The top-10-targeted
diagnostic uses the enumerated ranking and is therefore not a scalable
parameter-setting strategy. Reported time-to-target values exclude the offline
preprocessing that dominates the real cost, and meaningful comparison with
classical optimization requires accounting for that cost
\cite{Abbas2024,Bernal2024-benchmarking}. Pool membership is not a feasibility
certificate unless the pool is exhaustive. Finally, several provenance items,
including optimized VQE parameters and a hardware calibration snapshot, were not
retained, which limits exact reproduction of the stochastic campaigns; the
artifact bundle supports inspection and rescoring of the cached samples but not
full regeneration without rerunning the scripts.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:ds-mfg-conclusion}

For this drug-substance manufacturing case study, QAOA and VQE samples become
interpretable as process-design decisions only after the QUBO auxiliary
variables are separated from the flow variables and repaired exactly. With that
bridge in place, energy-targeted transferred-angle QAOA concentrated probability
on the optimal flowsheet (664 of 262144 ideal reads), an oracle-aided diagnostic
concentrated it further (1007 reads), and a selected-seed VQE follow-up reached
the optimum 20 times in 524288 reads. A small IBM hardware run completed the same
pipeline and found a near-optimal pooled flow but no optimum. None of these
outperform a simple classical hill climber or the exact solver.

The lesson for the process systems engineering community is that the engineering
effort between a discrete-design model and a usable quantum sample is
substantial and easy to understate, and that honest evaluation of gate-based
quantum optimization for process design requires scoring in the original
decision space and reporting the full preprocessing cost. The evidence here
supports feasibility and interpretation, not a claim of quantum speedup.
